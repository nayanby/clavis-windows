Recognizing the phenotype

  • Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM): ventricular thickening that is not adequately explained by hypertension, valve disease or another loading condition.
  • Dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM): left ventricular enlargement with impaired systolic function. Exclude coronary disease and loading conditions sufficient to explain the abnormality.
  • Non-dilated LV cardiomyopathy: nonischemic scar or fatty replacement without ventricular enlargement, or otherwise unexplained global systolic dysfunction without scar.
  • Restrictive cardiomyopathy: stiff ventricles impair filling and enlarge the atria. Ejection fraction (EF) may remain preserved despite substantial congestion.

Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy

Underlying disease

  • Sarcomeric HCM: commonly involves MYH7 or MYBPC3 and is often autosomal dominant with variable expression. Myocyte disarray and fibrosis contribute to stiffness and arrhythmia.
  • Preserved or high EF: does not mean normal function. A small, stiff ventricle can have impaired filling and cause heart failure despite apparently vigorous contraction.

Diagnosis and outflow obstruction

  • Diagnosis: in adults, unexplained LV wall thickness ≥15 mm supports HCM. Thickness of 13–14 mm may be significant with a family history or pathogenic variant; children need size-adjusted criteria.
  • Obstruction: an LV outflow tract (LVOT) gradient ≥30 mmHg defines obstruction. A gradient ≥50 mmHg becomes important when symptoms persist despite treatment and advanced therapy is being considered.
  • Why the gradient changes: reduced ventricular filling, reduced afterload or increased contractility can worsen obstruction. This explains why standing and Valsalva usually increase the murmur.
  • Mitral involvement: systolic anterior motion of the mitral valve contributes to obstruction and mitral regurgitation. Exercise echo can uncover obstruction that is absent at rest.
  • Apical HCM: may produce giant negative T waves. MRI is useful when apical thickening or an apical aneurysm is poorly seen on echocardiography.

Treating symptoms

  • First treatment: a nonvasodilating beta blocker usually reduces symptoms from obstruction. Avoid dehydration and unnecessary drugs that markedly reduce filling or afterload.
  • Alternatives: verapamil or diltiazem may be used when beta blockade is unsuitable or ineffective. Verapamil is unsafe with hypotension, severe resting symptoms or very high gradients.
  • Persistent symptoms: consider a myosin inhibitor, disopyramide with AV nodal blockade, or septal reduction. Mitral or papillary muscle abnormalities needing repair favor surgical myectomy.
  • Mavacamten: check interactions and monitor EF. Initiation with EF <55% is not recommended; interrupt treatment if EF falls below 50% or heart failure develops.

Arrhythmia and sudden death

  • Secondary prevention: previous cardiac arrest or sustained ventricular arrhythmia generally warrants ICD assessment after considering reversible causes.
  • Primary prevention: assess arrhythmic syncope, family HCM-related sudden death, wall thickness ≥30 mm, apical aneurysm and EF <50%. Nonsustained VT and extensive MRI scar add risk information.
  • Atrial fibrillation: clinical AF generally warrants anticoagulation even with a low conventional stroke-risk score. Treat stroke risk separately from outflow obstruction.

Dilated cardiomyopathy

Causes and heart failure treatment

  • Reversible contributors: assess alcohol, sustained tachyarrhythmia, cardiotoxic drugs and inflammation. Genetic susceptibility can coexist with acquired injury; alcohol-related disease requires abstinence.
  • Core treatment: use ARNI/ACE inhibitor/ARB, an evidence-based beta blocker, a mineralocorticoid antagonist and an SGLT2 inhibitor as tolerated. Diuretics relieve congestion; EF recovery alone does not justify withdrawing therapy.

Inherited arrhythmic risk

  • TTN truncating variants: are the most common genetic cause of DCM. Interpret the specific variant with the phenotype and pedigree; finding a TTN variant alone does not establish disease.
  • LMNA disease: conduction abnormalities and atrial or ventricular arrhythmias may precede severe dysfunction. An EF above 35% does not establish low risk; include genotype, nonsustained VT, block and scar in ICD assessment.
  • Other higher-risk genetic forms: pathogenic variants in FLNC, RBM20, PLN and desmosomal genes can increase arrhythmic risk. Device decisions may therefore require more than a generic EF cutoff.

Peripartum cardiomyopathy

  • Recognition: otherwise unexplained systolic heart failure near late pregnancy or postpartum, usually with EF <45%. Ventricular enlargement is not required.
  • Treatment: avoid ACE inhibitors, ARBs and ARNIs during pregnancy. Postpartum choices depend on lactation; selected bromocriptine protocols also require attention to anticoagulation.

Arrhythmogenic cardiomyopathy

  • Right ventricular disease: regional akinesia/dyskinesia, structural dysfunction, anterior T-wave inversion and ventricular arrhythmias support the diagnosis. Combine imaging, electrical, family and genetic findings rather than relying on one feature.
  • Genetic patterns: PKP2 commonly causes RV-predominant disease. DSP often causes LV-predominant disease with subepicardial or ring-like scar and myocarditis-like episodes.
  • Exercise risk: high-intensity endurance exercise can accelerate disease and arrhythmia, even before overt dysfunction. A normal echo does not eliminate inherited risk.

Restriction versus constriction

Both cause impaired filling and congestion. Restriction arises in the myocardium; constriction arises in the pericardium.

Important limits: normal pericardial thickness does not exclude constriction. A dip-and-plateau tracing or equalized diastolic pressures alone cannot establish the diagnosis.

Cardiac amyloidosis

Recognizing and typing amyloid

  • Clinical clues: increased wall thickness, conduction disease, carpal tunnel syndrome, neuropathy, low voltage or apical-sparing strain raise suspicion. None is independently diagnostic.
  • Screen for AL: request serum free light chains, serum immunofixation and urine immunofixation. Electrophoresis alone is insufficient; kidney dysfunction affects light-chain interpretation.
  • Nonbiopsy ATTR diagnosis: requires a compatible cardiac phenotype, grade 2–3 bone-tracer myocardial uptake confirmed appropriately with SPECT, and exclusion of monoclonal light-chain disease.
  • With a monoclonal abnormality: a positive PYP scan cannot establish ATTR alone. Tissue confirmation and reliable amyloid typing may be needed before selecting treatment.
  • Additional ATTR clues: spontaneous biceps tendon rupture and lumbar spinal stenosis can precede cardiac recognition. A negative fat-pad biopsy does not exclude cardiac amyloidosis.
  • Typing pitfall: a monoclonal protein does not itself prove AL, because a plasma-cell clone may coexist with ATTR. Identify the deposited protein when the nonbiopsy pathway is not valid.

Treatment follows the protein type

  • AL amyloidosis: needs urgent plasma-cell-directed treatment.
  • ATTR cardiomyopathy: tafamidis/acoramidis stabilize transthyretin; vutrisiran suppresses its production where indicated. Genetic testing distinguishes hereditary from wild-type ATTR.

Storage disorders and cardiac iron

Causes of ventricular thickening

  • Fabry disease: GLA-related alpha-galactosidase A deficiency causes LVH with neuropathic pain, angiokeratomas, reduced sweating, renal disease or stroke.
    • Enzyme activity may be normal in affected women, so genetic confirmation matters. Treatment includes enzyme replacement or a chaperone for an amenable variant.
  • PRKAG2 syndrome: altered AMPK signaling causes glycogen accumulation. LVH, pre-excitation and progressive conduction disease form the characteristic combination.
  • Danon disease: X-linked LAMP2 disease causes severe cardiomyopathy, skeletal myopathy, elevated CK and pre-excitation; learning or intellectual difficulties may accompany it. Early advanced-heart-failure assessment may be needed.

Myocardial iron

  • At standard 1.5-T MRI: T2* <20 ms indicates cardiac iron overload; <10 ms indicates severe overload. Lower T2* means more myocardial iron.
  • Assessment and treatment: ferritin alone cannot quantify cardiac deposition. Use cardiac imaging to guide appropriate chelation and follow-up.

Myocarditis

  • MRI findings: edema with nonischemic injury, often subepicardial inferolateral enhancement, supports myocarditis in the right clinical setting. Troponin elevation or normal coronaries alone does not establish it.
  • When biopsy matters: shock, rapid deterioration, serious VT or advanced block require urgent assessment. Biopsy is especially useful when the inflammatory subtype will change treatment.
  • Giant-cell myocarditis: rapidly progressive failure, ventricular arrhythmia and block warrant consideration. Biopsy establishes the diagnosis; treatment usually requires combined immunosuppression and early support/transplant assessment, rather than steroids alone.

Immune-mediated forms

  • Eosinophilic myocarditis: consider drug hypersensitivity or systemic eosinophilic disease when the clinical setting fits. Identifying the subtype matters because immunosuppression may be indicated.
  • Checkpoint-inhibitor myocarditis: can overlap with myositis and myasthenia-like weakness. This combination requires urgent cardiac, respiratory and neurological assessment and coordinated immunosuppressive treatment.
  • Steroid use: is cause-dependent. Immunosuppression for giant-cell, eosinophilic, sarcoid or checkpoint-inhibitor disease should not be generalized to every suspected viral myocarditis.

Cardiac sarcoidosis

  • Presentation: unexplained high-grade AV block, VT or ventricular dysfunction may be the first manifestation. Recognized extracardiac sarcoidosis may be absent.
  • Complementary imaging: CMR late gadolinium enhancement identifies injury/scar; properly prepared FDG-PET assesses active inflammation. Patchy disease makes an unguided biopsy less sensitive, so a negative result does not exclude involvement.
  • Treatment decisions: assess active disease for immunosuppression and assess pacing/ICD needs separately. Suppressing inflammation does not remove established arrhythmogenic scar.

Takotsubo syndrome

Recognition and complications

  • Presentation: transient apical or other ballooning often extends beyond one coronary territory. A stress trigger is common but not obligatory; exclude acute coronary syndrome and myocarditis.
  • Acute risk: reversibility does not prevent shock, ventricular thrombus or arrhythmia during the acute phase.

Shock treatment depends on the mechanism

  • With LVOT obstruction: positive inotropes can worsen obstruction. Carefully selected fluid may help when filling is inadequate and pulmonary congestion is absent.
  • Other mechanisms: pump failure, RV involvement and severe mitral regurgitation need different support. Do not give beta blockers indiscriminately during hemodynamic instability.

  • Do not confuse mapping measures: T1 helps characterize tissue composition; T2 reflects edema; T2* is used to quantify cardiac iron. Interpret each with the imaging protocol and clinical findings.

Family screening and exercise

Family assessment

  • Pathogenic/likely pathogenic familial variant: offer counseling, targeted cascade testing and appropriate clinical screening to first-degree relatives.
  • Variant of uncertain significance: does not by itself justify predictive family testing or irreversible treatment. Expert segregation studies may clarify it; continue surveillance guided by phenotype and family history.

Activity and athletic adaptation

  • Exercise advice: individualize activity in HCM; high-intensity endurance sport is particularly concerning in arrhythmogenic disease. Persistent ventricular arrhythmia after myocarditis prevents clearance for intensive sport.
  • Trabeculation: prominent trabeculations alone do not establish cardiomyopathy in an athlete with reassuring function, rhythm assessment and family evaluation.
